What Is CFD Trading? Contracts for Difference Explained Simply

A CFD lets you trade the price movement of an asset — an index, commodity, stock or crypto — without owning it. Here is how CFDs work, what they cost, and the risks every beginner must understand.

What exactly is a CFD?

A contract for difference (CFD) is an agreement between you and a broker to exchange the difference in an asset's price between when you open and close the position. You never own the underlying asset — you are trading its price movement only.

This structure makes it simple to trade global indices like the S&P 500, commodities like gold and oil, individual stocks and cryptocurrencies from a single account, both long and short.

What does CFD trading cost?

The main cost is the spread — the gap between buy and sell prices. Positions held overnight incur a financing charge called swap, which can be significant over weeks. Some account types charge a fixed commission with tighter spreads instead.

Always check the contract specification: contract size, margin requirement, swap rates and trading hours vary by instrument and broker.

What are the main risks?

Leverage is the biggest risk. Controlling a large position with a small margin means a small adverse move can wipe out the margin. Stop-loss orders, modest position sizes and awareness of gap risk around news are essential.

Regulation matters too: client fund segregation and negative balance protection differ by broker and jurisdiction. Verify how your broker handles both before depositing.

Pertanyaan Umum

›What is the difference between CFDs and buying stocks?

Buying a stock gives you ownership, dividends and voting rights. A stock CFD only mirrors the price (dividends are usually adjusted in cash), lets you short easily, and uses leverage — with the corresponding risk.

›Can I lose more than I deposit with CFDs?

It depends on the broker. Some jurisdictions require negative balance protection for retail clients; others do not. Check this in the broker's terms before trading.

›Are CFDs good for long-term investing?

Generally no. Overnight financing costs accumulate, making CFDs better suited to short- and medium-term positions. Long-term investors usually prefer owning the asset directly.
